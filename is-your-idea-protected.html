Is your idea protected when you submit it?

Almost certainly not — and the document people usually check to find out is the wrong document entirely.

1. A privacy policy is not a confidentiality agreement

This is the misunderstanding the whole category runs on. Someone is about to submit an idea, they feel a flicker of doubt, they go and read the privacy policy, they see reassuring language about encryption and security, and they submit.

But the two documents do opposite jobs.

Privacy policyNDA
What it does Discloses what the company may do with data Restricts what the recipient may do
Written to protect The company You, the disclosing party
Creates a duty of confidence? No Yes
Is it negotiated? No — unilateral, and revisable by them Yes — bilateral, and fixed once executed
If it’s broken, you get A regulatory complaint, maybe A contract claim, injunction, damages

A privacy policy is a compliance disclosure. Reading one to learn whether your idea is safe is like reading a restaurant’s allergen sheet to find out whether they’ll steal your recipe.

There is a second, sharper problem. A privacy policy is a document the company can rewrite. Most reserve exactly that right, with notice or sometimes without. An executed NDA cannot be revised unilaterally — it governs your disclosure on the terms that existed when you made it, permanently.

2. Privacy law doesn’t even cover your idea

GDPR, CCPA, and their relatives govern personal information — data about an identifiable human being. Your name is personal information. Your email is. Your IP address is.

Your business idea is not. It’s commercial information about a venture, not about a person. So it largely sits outside the protective scope of privacy law and of the privacy policy written to comply with it. Whatever protections that document offers, they were never aimed at the thing you care about.

This is why "we are GDPR compliant" tells you nothing about whether your idea is safe. Both statements can be entirely true at once: they comply fully with privacy law, and they owe your idea nothing.

3. Ideas have no default protection

People tend to assume some baseline protection exists. It doesn’t.

  • Copyright protects expression, not ideas. This isn’t a nuance — 17 U.S.C. §102(b) says so explicitly. Your written description may be copyrighted; the concept it describes is not.
  • Patents protect inventions, but only once filed. Before filing you have no patent rights, and public disclosure can start clocks running against you or bar protection outright in jurisdictions without a grace period.
  • Trade secret law is the only doctrine that covers a raw idea. Under the Defend Trade Secrets Act and state versions of the UTSA, it protects information that derives value from being secret — but only where the holder takes “reasonable measures to keep it secret.”

That last clause is where everything turns.

4. Why submitting can leave you worse off than silence

Disclosing to a party under no duty of confidence doesn’t merely fail to protect your idea. It can defeat the reasonable-measures element and destroy the trade-secret status you already had.

This is the part almost nobody is told. The risk isn’t only that a platform behaves badly. It’s that the act of submitting — voluntarily, to a recipient who owes you nothing — is itself evidence that you weren’t taking reasonable measures to keep the thing secret.

And trade secret status, once gone, does not come back. You can’t re-secret information. It’s a one-way door, and the submission form is the door.

Disclose the same idea under a signed NDA and the analysis inverts completely: you disclosed under an obligation of confidence, which is itself a reasonable measure and tends to support the protection rather than defeat it.

5. What to look for before you type anything

Read the terms and the privacy policy of any platform you're about to submit to, and look specifically for these. The first is the one that matters most:

  • Is there a confidentiality obligation at all? Search the terms for “confidential”. If the only hits are about their confidential information, or about not sending sensitive data over insecure channels, you have your answer.
    Do not stop at “the word appears, so I’m covered.” Read which direction each obligation points. A clause saying you must keep something secret is not a clause saying they must.
  • What licence do you grant them? Look for “you grant”. Words like perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, sublicensable, and transferable are each doing real work. Together they mean the grant survives you leaving.
  • Do they train on your input? Look for “train”, “fine-tune”, “improve our services”, and “Input and Output”. Training is not reversible: once your idea is in a model’s weights, deleting your account does nothing.
  • What happens if they’re acquired or fail? Look for “merger”, “acquisition”, “asset sale”, “bankruptcy”. Nearly every privacy policy permits transfer of your data in these events. Ask yourself who might end up holding it.
  • Who owns what gets built? If the platform builds something for you, does ownership vest in you, or do you get a licence to something they own?
  • Is there a cut of your revenue, and whose account does money land in? If payments route through the platform’s payment processor rather than yours, look for holds, minimums, and monthly withdrawal caps.
  • Can you get everything out, and what does it cost? Especially after you stop paying. Export that disappears on cancellation is not export.
  • How long do they keep it? “As long as reasonably necessary” means indefinitely, at their discretion.

6. What actually helps

  1. Get a confidentiality obligation in place before you disclose. Mutual if you can. Executed, dated, and retained — not a checkbox.
  2. File a provisional patent application if the idea is patentable. It is comparatively cheap, gives you twelve months, and lets you disclose from a position of priority. An NDA is not a substitute for this.
  3. Keep dated records. What you conceived, when, and what you disclosed to whom. Contemporaneous records decide these disputes.
  4. Disclose in layers. The problem to be solved, before the mechanism that solves it. Not everyone needs the whole thing on day one.
  5. Check what you can verify, not what you’re told. Anyone can write “your data is safe with us” on a landing page. Ask what would let you prove a breach if one happened.
